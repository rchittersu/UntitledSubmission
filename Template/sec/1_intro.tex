\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

Defocus blur is an unavoidable consequence of finite depth of field. Wide apertures are used to
gather light, to freeze motion, or for subject isolation, and whenever the scene spans more depth
than the lens can keep sharp, regions away from the focal plane are imaged through circles of
confusion (CoC) whose size grows with their distance from that plane. Single-image defocus
deblurring aims to recover the all-in-focus image and has progressed rapidly with learned
models~\cite{abuolaim2020dpdd,son2021kpac,lee2021ifan,ruan2022drbnet,quan2023nrknet,zamir2022restormer,ruan2023lakdnet}.

These models are developed at a resolution far below that of the images they are meant for.
Interchangeable-lens cameras record $24$--$60$\,MP and smartphone main sensors $50$--$200$\,MP, but
the standard DPDD benchmark~\cite{abuolaim2020dpdd} is distributed and evaluated at
$1680\times1120$, although it was captured at the $6720\times4480$ resolution of a Canon EOS~5D
Mark~IV. Downscaling by a factor $s$ shrinks every CoC by $s$, so the benchmark poses a much easier
problem than the photograph the user took: blur kernels are small relative to the network's receptive
field and training crops, and the finest detail, which is the hardest to restore, has already been
discarded. How to obtain the all-in-focus image at the native resolution of the sensor has, to our
knowledge, not been studied.

We find that the two obvious ways to apply existing methods at native resolution both fail, for
different reasons (\cref{fig:teaser}, \cref{sec:exp}).
\emph{Native-resolution inference}, necessarily in tiles, changes almost nothing: on the $76$ DPDD
test scenes at $30$\,MP, four recent deblurring networks stay within $0.2$\,dB of the blurry input
and the fifth~\cite{seizinger2025bokehlicious} gains $0.6$\,dB, while the same networks gain
$1.4$--$2.4$\,dB when the image is downscaled by $4$. Tile size and test-time statistics
correction~\cite{chu2022tlc} make no difference. The cause is not missing context or tile seams but
blur of an extent the networks never saw.
\emph{Deblurring at low resolution and upsampling} recovers the structure (\eg, $+1.5$\,dB over the
input with bicubic upsampling) but none of the native detail, and the result is visibly soft.
Restoring that detail with super-resolution is not a remedy: learned upsamplers add little over
bicubic, and generative ones~\cite{wang2024stablesr,yu2024supir,wu2024osediff} invent detail,
rewriting text into different glyphs and degrading regions that were already in focus
(up to $-1.6$\,dB in the focal plane for a one-step diffusion upsampler).

Our key observation is that the native-resolution observation is far from uninformative, and that
what it lacks locally it often contains elsewhere. Defocus varies across the image: in-focus regions
retain their full detail, mildly defocused regions retain part of it, and only strongly defocused
regions have lost it. Photographs are also highly self-similar~\cite{glasner2009single,zontak2011internal}:
the wall, fabric or foliage that is blurred in the background frequently appears sharp nearer the
focal plane. A deblurred low-resolution image tells us \emph{what} is where; the in-focus parts of
the native observation tell us \emph{what it looks like} at full resolution. Even a non-learned
version of this idea, which pastes the high band of the best-matching in-focus patch into defocused
regions, improves perceptual quality at almost no fidelity cost (\cref{sec:exp_analysis}).

We build on this observation with \method, a blur-guided upsampler that lifts any low-resolution
deblurring method to native resolution. It takes the native blurry image, the low-resolution
deblurred \emph{anchor}, and a \emph{blur map}, and works patch-wise. Three mechanisms shape the
output. (i)~A \emph{copy path} reproduces the observation where it is sharp, so in-focus content is
never re-synthesized. (ii)~An \emph{in-focus exemplar memory}, built once per image from sharp
regions anywhere in the photograph, is queried by every patch through the anchor, in which sharp and
defocused regions are comparable; relevant exemplars supply native texture, and a learned
``no match'' option lets the model abstain. Because the memory is global, each patch draws on the
whole image, and patch-wise inference is no longer limited by its tile. (iii)~A generator fills in
what neither path provides. We present a strong feed-forward variant and a one-step
diffusion-transformer variant that adds a generative prior while keeping the copy path in pixel
space. Because the upsampler depends on the low-resolution stage only through the anchor and the
blur map, any existing deblurring method can be plugged in without retraining; the blur map can be
taken from dual-pixel data when available or predicted from the anchor and the input otherwise.

Evaluating at native resolution raises its own difficulties. The full-resolution DPDD data exist only
as raw captures, so we develop them and calibrate each capture to the official rendering
($39$\,dB agreement at the benchmark resolution), register the f/22 targets to the f/4 inputs with a
translation-only model (a homography over-fits the blur difference), and stratify all results by the
local amount of defocus measured from the dual-pixel views. Headline metrics are standard and fixed
before any result of our method was available; blur-stratified, in-focus-preservation and
hallucination analyses explain the differences between methods.

In summary, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An analysis of defocus deblurring at native sensor resolution, showing on $30$\,MP data that
        native inference is close to the identity and that low-resolution deblurring followed by
        (generative) super-resolution either loses or invents detail, and why.
  \item \method, a blur-guided upsampler with a copy path and a global in-focus exemplar memory that
        transfers real texture across the whole image, in a feed-forward and a one-step
        diffusion-transformer variant, which lifts any low-resolution method to native resolution.
        \todo{headline numbers: +X dB / DISTS / human preference over the strongest baseline; N

  \item A native-resolution DPDD benchmark rebuilt from raw ($76$ test, $74$ validation, $350$
        training scenes) with registration, dual-pixel blur stratification and a fixed protocol, and
        an evaluation of $5$ deblurring networks, $5$ upsamplers and $5$ training-free fusion
        baselines on it.
\end{itemize}
